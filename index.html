Case Study WellSky · Specialty Care

The division's largest revenue-cycle migration

Senior Solutions Analyst at WellSky and the solutions point of contact on the program, forward-deployed as the hub between Solutions, Engineering, Professional Services, PM, and the client.

Impact

15+ financial and compliance reports and 3 bill forms, all net-new for this migration. The platform now runs high-volume, mixed-model authorization billing with step-downs and intricate requirements.

Challenge

A Fortune 500 post-acute provider needed to move its billing off a legacy system onto WellSky Specialty Care. Requirements changed week to week, and reporting definitions weren't settled until late.

My role

  • Run the team's backlog. I write its Jira stories and prioritize the work for a team of four developers and an engineering lead, taking implementation requirements from Salesforce intake to production in weeks.
  • Defined eligibility gating and a new hour-based authorization and reimbursement engine (day, week, and total-hour limits across step-downs and rollups).
  • Specified a bi-directional, real-time authorization-sync API between the two platforms.
  • Authored 50+ billing rules across CMS-1500 and UB-04 and 3 new bill forms. Wrote the acceptance criteria and testing scenarios that supported go-live readiness.

Forward-deployed with the client

I am embedded with the client, running biweekly working sessions and carrying their decisions into engineering as the point of contact between the client and the build. If requirements are ambiguous, I build mockups with AI to settle it before writing the ticket. I also own release communications, every release note and user-guide update, including the prospect-facing docs.

The difficult systems

  • Multi-level, hour-based authorization engine. Total-hour limits sit above per-week, which sit above per-day, with hours that can be shared across a group of disciplines for the patient. Getting the precedence and the step-down configuration right is what decides whether a visit is authorized, whether it can be billed, and whether my client gets paid.
  • Tiered, configurable reimbursement engine. Rates vary by program, payer, and service line, so the engine had to be data-driven rather than hard-coded, with the tiers and contract variables exposed as configuration the client could perform themselves. It's a multi-tenant platform, so I often have to decide how a change affects my client while keeping the platform as a whole in mind, and what opening up a capability means for them and for others.
  • Authorization-sync API. I specified the updates to the bi-directional, real-time API keeping authorizations in sync between the two systems – specifying field mappings, sync triggers, and conflict behavior, including mapping each discipline to the correct procedure codes.

Tracking the money from billed to paid reimbursement

  • End-to-end authorization tracking. Complex authorizations need full visibility from charges to bills to payments. I designed the tracking that keeps each claim accurate as charges roll up into one code and bill out as timed units, so the amount owed is correctly billed and reimbursed.
  • Two reimbursement-review reports, built from scratch with the client. Each compares expected reimbursement against what actually landed on the claim. Every column is a claim-relevant field or value, built with the client, several calculated from existing data to surface insights the client didn't have before. Operations and billing review differently, so I split the work into an hourly authorization report for operations and a claim review report for billing, each with its own submission criteria.
  • A charge inquiry window I designed. Sort and pick a bill, and a pop-out shows exactly which charges hit it and traces each one back to the General Ledger.

Go-live support and ongoing testing

I owned testing readiness for the cutover, providing many pages and screenshots of explanatory notes and testing scenarios for their go-live facilities; I also facilitated off-cycle release to solve go-live issues at live sites in an urgent manner.

Research Raven · 2026

Raven, a technology atlas ↗

A research site that measures how far an idea has moved toward a product from the public record alone, with every number printed beside its denominator and the rule that produced it.

Impact

One published chain, orbital debris and four downstream products, with every score's components printed beside it and an evidence score that never calls itself a probability. The same machinery measures the next chain.

Challenge

I wanted to say how far a technology has moved toward market using only public data, without mistaking the whole literature growing for a boom, a keyword match for relevance, or a score for a forecast.

What I did

  • Measured four layers by stated rules: research counted per year beside the whole index, with funding and regulation alongside; citation dependency with a negative control; hand-checked barriers typed by whether they can lift; and speculation kept apart and labelled.
  • Fixed two controls before any chain ran, so a product's growth is a position between a literature that matured and one that boomed, never a bare number.
  • Built a hand-checked truth set, negatives included, that every automation is scored against. The keyword baseline recovered 1 of 7 barriers; the model layer is built and has not yet run.
